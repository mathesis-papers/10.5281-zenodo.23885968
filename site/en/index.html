OEIS A391810: formula (5), COMMENTS (C.1)--(C.10), and prime-index vanishing

Haobo Ma, Wenlin Zhang

Paper

Paul D. Hanna’s OEIS entry A391810 asks why a complicated array of signed integers has surprisingly simple totals and zeros at certain positions. Haobo Ma and Wenlin Zhang answer by recovering each row from a finite polynomial, which explains the totals and proves several predicted entry formulas. They also prove that prime-numbered positions beyond the row number are zero, but not that the next prime always marks the first zero.

The construction allows positive and negative powers of a bookkeeping symbol x. For a positive integer n, take x raised to j times (one minus x raised to j) raised to n+j, and sum over all integers j. The entry T(n,k) records the coefficient of x raised to −k, for nonnegative integers k. These are formal Laurent series: numerical convergence is not the issue. The authors show that only finitely many summands affect each coefficient and that all powers have a common lower bound. This makes the definition meaningful. Hanna’s questions concern cancellations among the coefficients, not numerical values of the infinite expression.

The decisive simplification collects the nonpositive powers using y to represent x⁻¹. The resulting polynomial starts with −1 and, for each integer h from 1 through n, includes yʰ multiplied by n−h copies of 1−yʰ. Its coefficient of yᵏ is exactly T(n,k). Positive j contribute only positive powers of x; sufficiently negative j do likewise. The boundary term contributes precisely the constant −1. The polynomial also bounds the row: entries beyond the square of the integer part of (n+1)/2 vanish. For even n, this sharper bound requires pairing and canceling highest-degree terms, rather than merely bounding each summand separately.

Setting y to −1 in this finite polynomial, not the infinite series, gives the alternating coefficient sum. Odd h give negative contributions whose magnitudes are powers of two; even h contribute zero except for a possible final term. Adding gives the negative alternating row total as 2(2ⁿ−(−1)ⁿ)/3. This is twice the Jacobsthal number Jₙ: these numbers start with 0 and 1, and each subsequent number is the previous one plus twice its predecessor. Equivalently, Jₙ is the nearest integer to 2ⁿ/3. These Jacobsthal descriptions were already known; their connection with Hanna’s rows is the result at issue. For n=3, the row is −1, 1, −1, 2, −1. Its ordinary sum is zero, while reversing the sign of its alternating sum gives 6, twice J₃. The result requires n≥1; the same finite expression at zero would not satisfy it.

Expanding the polynomial explains individual entries. The hth piece contains only powers with exponents divisible by h, so, for positive k, only divisors h of k contribute to T(n,k). Each contribution has a binomial coefficient counting choices of k/h−1 factors from n−h factors, positive for an even number chosen and negative for an odd number; impossible choices contribute zero. This proves Hanna’s ten predictions about initial entries, entries at and just before the row endpoint, and odd-prime diagonal entries. For an odd prime p, T(p,p)=2 because both divisors contribute one. For any prime q>n, only divisor 1 is eligible, but it demands too many choices, so T(n,q)=0.

The polynomial also yields other conjectured summaries. Evaluating at 1 gives row sum zero; differentiating gives the position-weighted sum 1. Weighting by squared positions gives 5−2n for n≥2. Differentiation at −1 produces alternating position-weighted totals, and the authors derive a rational generating function packaging them. Several separate observations thus follow from one representation.

The manuscript supplies written proofs and reports supplementary evidence. It cites work by the Trureturing contributors providing Lean sources for the alternating-total identity, its original-series interpretation, rounding relation, coefficient bridge and support bound, and for the ordinary row sum and first two weighted sums. This reported Lean scope does not include the divisor formula, ten entry predictions, prime-index vanishing or alternating weighted generating function. Reported exact computations compare complete rows through 79 and check the Jacobsthal scalar identity through 512; finite checks are not universal proofs.

The remaining distinction concerns the first zero. For n≥5, Hanna predicts that every position before the first prime greater than n is nonzero. Proving that the prime position vanishes does not exclude earlier composite-position zeros: several divisor contributions might cancel. The authors report agreement for rows 5 through 79, but leave the general assertion open. Their contribution unifies these nonpositive Laurent coefficients and partially settles the zero-location question; the two other bilateral identities mentioned in the source entry remain outside its scope.
